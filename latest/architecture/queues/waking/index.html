    Waking

    A subscription claims while its claims come back full, and otherwise waits its poll interval, which a publish of the same broker or a notification cuts short.

    A subscription claims when the runtime polls its stream. A claim that filled its limit is followed by the next one at once, so a backlog drains without waiting. A claim that found fewer rows than it asked for means the queue is empty for now, and the subscription waits. A claim that fails reaches the stream as an error item, and the next one waits one second, so a database out of reach cannot spin the loop.

    A publish through the broker wakes the subscriptions of its table on the same broker: a Repository, a route, a reply, and a message a test publishes through the harness. In a table with groups it wakes the subscriptions of its own group alone. A subscription busy with a claim keeps the wake-up for its next wait, which then returns at once. The wake-up costs a publish one atomic operation per subscription it wakes, and a subscription nothing per message. A row the service writes itself, with its own SQL or through a handler's Tx, wakes nobody: it waits for the poll interval, or for a notification.

    LISTEN/NOTIFY​

    On PostgreSQL, listen_notify makes subscriptions in other processes wake too. The broker takes one connection of the pool at connect and keeps it until shutdown, listening there. Each subscription listens on its table's channel before its first claim: the table's name, qualified with its schema where it has one, as in app.jobs. Each publish of the broker runs SELECT pg_notify(channel, group) on the connection that wrote the row, right after the write; a notification with an empty payload wakes every subscription of the table. A notification that fails is logged, and the publish still succeeds: the poll interval reaches the row.

    PostgreSQL delivers a notification when its transaction commits, so a woken subscription finds the row visible. A notification sent while the listening connection was lost is gone: the broker connects again and wakes every subscription once. Channel names keep 63 bytes, so a subscription to a table whose qualified name is longer stops at startup. The switch costs one connection for the broker's life and one more statement per publish. A transaction that notifies also takes a lock global to the server at commit, so notifying commits run one after another, which limits many concurrent writers. On another database listen_notify does not compile.

    The server documents the mechanism on NOTIFY: delivery at commit, a payload under 8000 bytes, and the 63-byte limit on identifiers. The commit lock is described by the PostgreSQL developers on the mailing list.
